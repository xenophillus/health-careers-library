# Health Careers Library — Editorial standards

Audience: high-school students learning about healthcare careers. Each article should be a trustworthy, understandable reference and remain useful beyond any particular patient scenario.

## Source and verification requirements
1. Ground occupation-wide responsibilities in authoritative descriptions (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook, O*NET, professional associations, credentialing bodies, and applicable state licensing agencies). Verify source currency and record URLs in the Sources tab.
2. Separate national occupational descriptions from state-specific scope-of-practice or credential requirements. Use "may", "where permitted", and appropriate supervision qualifiers when responsibilities vary.
3. Never invent numbers, credentials, specific legal permissions, procedures, clinical independence, or job duties. Mark any unresolved claim for fact-checking.
4. Use neutral terminology that reflects the actual profession. Distinguish related professions and specialties where confusion is likely.

## Duties and Responsibilities
1. Start with a clear 1–2 sentence account of the profession's purpose.
2. Organize the **major functional domains** as short labeled bullets, with accessible explanations. Cover prevention, assessment, intervention, recovery, follow-up, coordination, patient safety, technical/administrative functions **when they are actually part of that occupation**. Do not force a generic clinical-care cycle onto nonclinical jobs.
3. Present broad responsibilities before examples; avoid a singular injury, patient scenario, machine, or procedure that might misrepresent the whole occupation.
4. Add a short explanation of scope, supervision, state variation, or specialization when relevant.
5. Keep the distinction between duties (what professionals do), A Day in the Life (illustrative workflow), Education and Training (preparation), and Licensure/Certification (legal and credential requirements).

## All sections
- Maintain all 15 required section headings with nonredundant substance.
- Explain field-specific terms briefly; favor precise, student-readable writing over either oversimplification or clinical jargon.
- Treat examples as illustrative rather than universal. Show range of settings and patient populations where supported.
- Prefer accurate, current, verifiable information. Date quantitative statistics and attribute them to primary sources.
- Do not equate a credential with legal permission to perform a clinical procedure. Respect differing state rules and employer protocols.
- Cite source material in each article's bibliography. Add appropriately licensed, credited, relevant imagery with meaningful alt text; log the image source URL, credit, license, and verification status in the Media tab.
- Never mark editorial drafts approved or publish them automatically. Review remains required.
