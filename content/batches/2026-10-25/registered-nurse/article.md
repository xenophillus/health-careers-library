## Career Overview

Registered nurses (RNs) combine direct patient care with responsibility for understanding a patient’s needs, planning nursing care, and checking whether it is helping. They connect observations at the bedside with information from the wider healthcare team. Teaching a family how to manage care at home can be as important as using equipment or giving a treatment.

Nursing has its own clinical judgment and responsibilities. In Kansas, the legal definition specifically distinguishes a nursing diagnosis—identifying a person’s responses and nursing needs—from a medical diagnosis. An RN license is also different from an advanced practice registered nurse license; becoming an RN does not by itself make someone a nurse practitioner.

## Duties and Responsibilities

RN responsibilities connect assessment, action, and follow-up:

- **Assessment and planning:** Gather information about symptoms, health history and daily functioning; identify nursing needs and help establish a plan of care.
- **Treatment and monitoring:** Carry out authorized medications and treatments, watch for responses, and report important changes.
- **Patient teaching:** Explain care in understandable language and check that patients or caregivers can use the information.
- **Coordination:** Share observations with physicians, therapists, pharmacists, and other nurses so care remains connected.
- **Records and supervision:** Document care and outcomes; undertake appropriate team supervision or delegation within the nurse’s role.

The central question is not simply whether a task was finished. The nurse must also notice whether the patient’s condition or needs have changed.

## A Day in the Life

Consider an illustrative hospital shift. After receiving a handoff, an RN reviews the care plans and meets assigned patients. One patient is preparing to go home, while another needs repeated monitoring. The nurse decides what needs attention first, rather than treating the assignment as a fixed checklist.

During care, a patient describes a new symptom. The RN gathers more information, communicates with the appropriate clinician, and updates the nursing plan. Later, the nurse helps the departing patient understand the next steps and checks for questions. Records and the next handoff preserve information for colleagues.

This example follows the nursing process: assess, plan, act, and evaluate. A community or school nurse applies the same reasoning to a different setting and rhythm.

## Work Environment and Schedule

Hospitals, outpatient practices, home health agencies, nursing facilities, and schools employ RNs. Hospital care continues around the clock, so positions may include nights, weekends, and holidays. An office or school role may follow a more regular schedule.

Setting changes the work itself. On a hospital unit, equipment and colleagues are close by, but several patients may need attention at once. In home care, the nurse sees how the person’s living environment affects care. A school setting places more emphasis on supporting health during the school day. Comparing settings is useful because “nurse” does not describe one universal workday.

## Skills, Knowledge, and Personal Qualities

RNs need to connect biological science with information from the person receiving care. A measurement matters, but so do what the patient says, what has changed, and whether the person can manage daily activities. Careful reasoning helps the nurse distinguish a routine need from a change requiring prompt attention.

Clear writing, listening, and teamwork are practical clinical skills. A vague handoff can leave a colleague without information needed for care. Patient teaching also requires adapting explanations instead of assuming familiarity with medical terms. Reliability includes recognizing uncertainty and seeking help before acting beyond one’s competence.

## Physical and Emotional Demands

Bedside work can involve standing, repeated movement, and helping patients change position or transfer. Safe patient handling depends on suitable equipment, trained coworkers, and workplace systems; it is not a test of whether a nurse is strong enough to lift someone alone. NIOSH identifies patient handling as a major source of work-related injury.

Nurses also encounter fear, pain, loss, and competing needs. Providing support while managing interruptions requires sustained attention. The demands differ between a busy inpatient unit and a scheduled outpatient service, making staffing, orientation, available help, and shift patterns meaningful questions when comparing jobs.

## Education and Training

Approved associate-degree, bachelor’s-degree, and diploma nursing routes can prepare graduates for RN licensure. A Bachelor of Science in Nursing (BSN) includes nursing science and clinical learning alongside broader study, including community health and leadership. Associate-degree graduates may later enter an RN-to-BSN program that builds on their earlier education.

For a high-school student, the key distinction is between a **prelicensure** program that prepares someone to become an RN and a degree-completion program for someone already licensed. A BSN completion program is not automatically an entry route for a student without nursing preparation.

Use the Kansas Board of Nursing’s approved-program list when considering Kansas schools. Examine prerequisites and the actual sequence of clinical study rather than assuming the degree name tells you the total time after high school. Biology, chemistry, mathematics, and communication provide useful preparation.

## Certification and Licensure

RNs need legal authorization from a nursing regulatory body. For an initial Kansas application, the Board’s checklist includes official transcripts, fingerprints/background-check materials, and NCLEX registration. RN applicants take the NCLEX-RN and must satisfy the Board’s other licensing requirements; a school diploma alone is not a nursing license.

Kansas participates in the Nurse Licensure Compact. A qualifying multistate license and a single-state license are different; multistate status is not automatic for every nurse.

The Kansas scope decision tree also considers the law, agency policies, education, demonstrated competence, and the patient’s needs. A license is not a promise that every RN can safely perform every nursing activity without additional preparation. Specialty certification recognizes additional qualifications and does not replace licensure.

## Salary, Entry-Level Earnings, and Job Outlook

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics reports a **May 2025 national median annual wage of $97,550** for registered nurses. Half earned more and half less. This is not a new-graduate salary or a Kansas wage estimate.

BLS projects **6% employment growth from 2025 to 2035** and approximately **180,800 annual openings**, averaged over that decade. Openings include replacement needs as well as newly created jobs. These statistics cover RNs, not nurse practitioners or nurse anesthetists.

When comparing offers, distinguish the base wage from extra pay for particular shifts, overtime, or temporary assignments. The national median does not establish what any one employer will pay.

## Specialties and Career Advancement

An RN may develop expertise with a patient group, a body system, or a care setting: for example, children, older adults, cancer care, emergency care, or community health. Specialty work means learning the needs and risks of that population, not just changing the job title.

Other directions include educating nurses, coordinating care, improving quality, or supervising a service. Further education can support these moves. RN-to-BSN and graduate programs serve different purposes; advanced practice roles require their own education and regulatory qualifications. A nurse can also develop deep clinical expertise while continuing direct care rather than moving into management.

## Benefits and Challenges of the Career

Nursing offers the opportunity to combine scientific reasoning with sustained human contact. A useful outcome may be a patient’s improved condition, a family’s growing confidence, or a care problem recognized before it worsens. The range of settings gives nurses ways to explore different kinds of work across a career.

Responsibility is also a substantial part of the job. Nurses must keep evaluating care despite interruptions and may face situations where several people need help at once. Shift schedules, patient-handling demands, and emotionally difficult care can affect work-life balance. Good teamwork and a workplace that supports safe care are important parts of the job, not optional extras.

## Career Connections and Related Occupations

**Licensed practical nurses** provide nursing care with a different preparation and scope; Kansas describes their work within supportive and restorative care under direction. **Nursing assistants** help with daily care but do not hold an RN license. **Nurse practitioners** are advanced practice nurses with additional preparation and legal authority.

RNs also work alongside physicians, pharmacists, physical therapists, and social workers. For example, the RN may notice that a discharge plan will be difficult for a patient to follow and bring that concern to the team. Comparing professions should include who assesses, who plans, and who follows up—not only which tasks look similar at the bedside.

## Could This Career Interest You?

Would you enjoy combining a conversation with clinical observations to understand what someone needs? Can you picture explaining care patiently and then checking whether your explanation worked? How do you feel about responsibility for noticing changes and speaking up?

Compare two nursing settings rather than deciding from television depictions of emergency rooms. An informational conversation with a school nurse and one with an inpatient nurse can reveal different schedules, relationships, and decisions. Ask what occupies most of the workday, which parts require the most judgment, and what helped during the transition from student to licensed nurse.

## Professional Associations

The [American Nurses Association (ANA)](https://www.nursingworld.org/ana/) provides professional resources, continuing education, and advocacy related to nursing practice and working conditions. Its nursing-process explanation is useful for understanding why RN work includes assessment and evaluation as well as treatment.

A professional association represents and supports the profession. The Kansas Board of Nursing regulates practice, and the NCLEX is a licensure examination. These organizations and functions are connected but are not interchangeable.
