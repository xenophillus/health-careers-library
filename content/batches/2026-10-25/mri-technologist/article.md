## Career Overview

Magnetic resonance imaging technologists create detailed images used to investigate disease and injury. MRI uses a strong magnetic field and radiofrequency energy, rather than X-rays, to produce information about tissues inside the body. The technologist combines scanner operation with patient preparation and careful safety screening.

MRI belongs to Diagnostic Services. A technologist obtains and checks the images; a radiologist interprets the examination. Helping a person remain comfortable and still can be as important to image quality as choosing the correct scanner settings. [ARRT’s MRI overview](https://www.arrt.org/pages/earn-arrt-credentials/credential-options/mri) describes both the technical role and the support patients may need.

## Duties and Responsibilities

MRI technologists organize each examination around the requested images and the safety of the MR environment. Major responsibilities include:

- **Review and screening:** Confirm the requested examination, review relevant history, and screen patients and items for MRI safety concerns.
- **Preparation and positioning:** Explain the scan, provide hearing protection, and position the patient with the appropriate imaging coil, an equipment component used to receive or transmit signals.
- **Image acquisition:** Run the examination’s imaging sequences and adjust technical choices within approved protocols.
- **Monitoring and quality:** Communicate with the patient during scanning, check images for quality, and respond to motion or other problems.
- **Records and equipment:** Document the examination, transfer images for interpretation, and report equipment faults.

Some examinations use injected contrast. Administration is subject to the technologist’s authorized scope and workplace procedures; not every MRI requires contrast. [O*NET](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/29-2035.00) describes these responsibilities.

## A Day in the Life

In an illustrative outpatient examination, the technologist reviews the order and completes screening before the patient enters the scanner area. The technologist explains the sounds and the need to remain still, provides hearing protection, and positions the patient and imaging coil.

At the console, the technologist watches the examination progress and communicates with the patient. If movement affects the images, the team may need to adjust the plan or repeat part of the examination. After scanning, the technologist checks that the requested images and records are complete before preparing for the next patient.

An inpatient examination may require more coordination around mobility and monitoring equipment. The same scanner does not make every patient’s visit identical. [ARRT’s MRI task inventory](https://assets-us-01.kc-usercontent.com/406ac8c6-58e8-00b3-e3c1-0c312965deb2/fb2e1193-26ce-4f5b-9da6-50e8abe9a94f/Magnetic%20Resonance%20Imaging%20Task%20Inventory%202025.pdf) connects preparation, image production, and patient care.

## Work Environment and Schedule

MRI technologists work in hospitals, imaging laboratories, physicians’ offices, and outpatient centers. Some departments mainly see scheduled appointments; others also support patients who are already in hospital and need coordinated transport and care. Most radiologic and MRI technologists work full time, and some shifts include evenings, weekends, or nights.

The scanner room and control area serve different functions. The technologist moves between direct patient preparation and console work, with access to the magnet area carefully controlled. The [FDA’s professional guidance](https://www.fda.gov/radiation-emitting-products/mri-magnetic-resonance-imaging/information-professionals) emphasizes knowing the site’s safety procedures and whom to contact when a concern arises.

## Skills, Knowledge, and Personal Qualities

MRI requires understanding anatomy, image formation, and the effect of scan settings on the information produced. The technologist must also notice motion or other image problems and distinguish a technical issue from a question requiring clinical review.

Clear communication helps patients manage noise, confined space, and the need to keep still. Good judgment includes checking information about an implanted device rather than assuming that every implant is either safe or unsafe. “MR Conditional” means a device can be used only under its specified conditions. [FDA guidance](https://www.fda.gov/radiation-emitting-products/mri-magnetic-resonance-imaging/information-professionals) explains why those conditions matter.

MRI training therefore develops both technical reasoning and patient communication. [Mayo Clinic’s MRI program](https://college.mayo.edu/academics/health-sciences-education/magnetic-resonance-imaging-mri-program-minnesota/) illustrates how classroom learning, laboratory practice, and supervised clinical experience support those skills.

## Physical and Emotional Demands

Technologists help patients onto and off the table and support people with limited mobility, pain, or anxiety. A person who feels enclosed in the scanner may need reassurance and communication throughout the examination. Safe transfers and positioning protect both the patient and staff.

MRI does not expose patients to ionizing radiation, but it has its own hazards. Strong magnetic fields can attract unsuitable objects; scanner noise can harm hearing without appropriate protection; radiofrequency energy can cause heating. These are reasons for careful screening and established safety practices, not reasons to treat the scanner as an ordinary room. The [FDA’s benefits-and-risks guidance](https://www.fda.gov/radiation-emitting-products/mri-magnetic-resonance-imaging/benefits-and-risks) explains these differences.

## Education and Training

MRI has more than one education route. A student can pursue an MRI program through ARRT’s primary pathway, which requires an associate degree or higher and completion of an ARRT-approved program in MRI. A person who already holds an eligible imaging credential can pursue the postprimary pathway with the required structured education and clinical experience.

This means MRI is not restricted to people who first worked in X-ray radiography. When comparing programs, identify which route each program supports and what preparation it expects before admission. A short program for experienced imaging workers is not necessarily a complete entry route after high school.

Clinical learning includes screening, patient positioning, equipment use, and image-quality assessment. [Mayo Clinic’s program](https://college.mayo.edu/academics/health-sciences-education/magnetic-resonance-imaging-mri-program-minnesota/) is one example of education combining classroom, lab, and clinical work; its structure should not be treated as the universal length or admission model for every MRI program.

## Certification and Licensure

**National credentials:** ARRT offers MRI certification and registration through both primary and postprimary pathways. Education, ethics, and examination requirements apply. The postprimary route requires an appropriate supporting credential; being employed somewhere in healthcare is not by itself sufficient. [Primary requirements](https://www.arrt.org/pages/earn-arrt-credentials/initial-requirements/primary-requirements) and [postprimary requirements](https://www.arrt.org/pages/earn-arrt-credentials/initial-requirements/postprimary-requirement) explain the distinction.

**Kansas:** K.S.A. 65-7302(m) explicitly excludes MRI, echocardiography, and diagnostic sonography from the definition of radiologic technology under the state’s radiologic-technologist licensing act. MRI therefore should not be described as requiring that act’s radiologic-technologist license. Employers can still require an appropriate MRI credential and demonstrated competence. National certification, employer qualifications, and the legal requirements for specific duties are separate matters.

This exclusion does not authorize unrelated X-ray procedures or every clinical task. A position that combines MRI with radiography must meet the requirements for the work actually performed.

## Salary, Entry-Level Earnings, and Job Outlook

BLS reports a **national median annual wage of $95,480 for magnetic resonance imaging technologists in May 2025** and projects **8% employment growth from 2025 to 2035**. These figures are for MRI technologists, not the combined radiologic-and-MRI category.

A median divides the workforce’s wages in half; it does not describe a guaranteed first salary. Compare actual offers by location, schedule, experience requirements, and benefits. BLS’s headline combined annual-openings figure covers both radiologic and MRI technologists and should not be read as MRI-only openings. Source: [BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/radiologic-technologists.htm).

## Specialties and Career Advancement

MRI practice can develop around different anatomy and clinical needs, including brain and spine, joints, body organs, or cardiovascular examinations. ARRT’s task inventory shows this range. Experience in a specialist service involves learning its imaging protocols and patient needs, rather than acquiring a different job title for each body part.

Experienced technologists may take on student instruction, quality work, or technical leadership. Research settings can also involve specialized imaging methods and coordinated study visits. [O*NET](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/29-2035.00) includes teaching, equipment troubleshooting, and research-subject scheduling among reported tasks. Moving into leadership or another imaging discipline requires the relevant preparation and demonstrated competence.

## Benefits and Challenges of the Career

MRI offers a combination of detailed technology and personal care. A technologist can see how careful preparation and communication improve the images available to the medical team. Someone interested in anatomy and computers may enjoy the challenge of understanding why images look the way they do.

The work also calls for sustained attention. A screening question that seems routine may reveal information essential to safety. A patient who cannot remain still can make an examination difficult, and the technologist must balance image quality with the person’s comfort and condition. The result depends on judgment and teamwork as well as the scanner.

## Career Connections and Related Occupations

MRI, CT, and radiography can all produce images of the same body region, but they use different physical methods and require distinct preparation. CT and conventional radiography use X-rays; MRI uses magnetic fields and radiofrequency energy. Sonographers use ultrasound, while nuclear medicine technologists work with radiopharmaceuticals and detectors.

Radiologists interpret medical images and work with technologists on examination needs. Medical physicists contribute expertise in imaging science and equipment performance. A student interested in images should compare whether the appeal is acquiring them, interpreting them as a physician, or working on the physics and technology behind them.

## Could This Career Interest You?

Would you enjoy detailed computer work while staying attentive to a person who cannot easily see you during the scan? Can you explain a noisy, unfamiliar examination calmly? Do you like investigating a technical problem and checking safety information before proceeding?

Explore both direct-entry MRI education and routes for already-qualified imaging professionals. Ask a program how students gain supervised screening and positioning experience, and ask technologists what changes between a routine outpatient scan and an inpatient examination.

## Professional Associations

The [International Society for MR Radiographers & Technologists (ISMRT)](https://www.ismrm.org/smrt/) focuses on the MR community. Its resources include education, safety information, meetings, and professional connections. It is a section of the International Society for Magnetic Resonance in Medicine.

The [American Society of Radiologic Technologists (ASRT)](https://www.asrt.org/main/about-asrt) also serves medical imaging professionals, including MRI technologists. Professional associations support learning and the profession; they are separate from ARRT’s credentialing function and from state regulation.
