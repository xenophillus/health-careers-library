## Career Overview

Cancer registrars build reliable information about cancer diagnoses, treatment, and outcomes. They review medical records and organize selected details into a cancer registry: an information system that makes individual cases useful for studying care and population health.

This is a specialized health information career. A registrar does not diagnose a tumor or choose treatment. Instead, the registrar makes sure the documented cancer story is captured accurately and consistently. Researchers, healthcare teams, and public health programs can then use registry information to examine cancer patterns, evaluate services, and plan prevention efforts.

## Duties and Responsibilities

Registry work follows a case over time, not just through a single bill or office visit. Major responsibilities include:

- Casefinding: Identify diagnoses that meet the registry's reporting rules so eligible cases are not missed.
- Abstracting: Gather relevant details from records, such as the cancer's location, diagnosis, extent, and initial treatment. An abstract is a structured summary of a case.
- Classification: Apply the registry's coding and staging rules to documented information, using the appropriate manuals and references.
- Quality review: Check completeness and consistency, resolve discrepancies, and combine information from different sources appropriately.
- Follow-up and reporting: Update required information and prepare data for the registry's reporting obligations and analyses.
- Confidentiality: Protect identifying information and follow approved procedures for data access and disclosure.

A missing treatment record and a record stating that no treatment occurred are different findings. Recognizing that difference is part of maintaining useful data.

## A Day in the Life

In an illustrative hospital workday, a registrar starts with a list of potential new cases. The registrar reviews a pathology report and checks whether the diagnosis meets the reporting rules. For a reportable case, the registrar gathers information from additional clinical documents and enters a structured summary.

A software check flags an inconsistency. Rather than forcing the record to pass, the registrar examines the source documents and consults the applicable reference or a colleague. Later work might include following up on missing treatment information and preparing a data submission. A central-registry registrar may spend more time reviewing reports received from multiple facilities and resolving possible duplicates.

## Work Environment and Schedule

Cancer registrars work in hospital registries and in central registries covering a state or other population. Special-purpose registries concentrate on particular cancers or populations. Hospital work focuses on the facility's cancer cases, while a population-based registry aims to bring together reportable cases from its geographic area.

The job involves substantial computer and document work. BLS describes the broader medical registrar and health information technologist group as mainly office-based and full time. Collaboration may involve other registrars, clinicians, records staff, and public health specialists. The balance between new cases, follow-up, quality checks, and reporting depends on the registry.

## Skills, Knowledge, and Personal Qualities

Cancer registration combines medical understanding with disciplined information work. Anatomy helps the registrar understand where a cancer started; knowledge of diagnostic and treatment terminology helps make sense of pathology, imaging, and clinical reports. The registrar also needs to learn the reporting and classification rules that make cases comparable.

Careful reading is essential when information is scattered across documents or appears inconsistent. Knowing when to consult an updated manual is more valuable than guessing from memory. Analytical skills help identify unusual patterns in data, and clear communication helps resolve questions with colleagues. Respect for confidentiality matters because a registry contains information about real people, not merely rows in a spreadsheet.

## Physical and Emotional Demands

The work requires sustained attention to detailed records and frequent use of a computer. It offers an information-based role in cancer care rather than the physical tasks of bedside treatment. A comfortable workstation and changes in posture matter during prolonged screen work.

Reviewing serious illness, recurrence, and death can have an emotional effect even without direct patient contact. Registrars also face the pressure of accurate, timely reporting and changing data requirements. It helps to value careful follow-through: unresolved discrepancies should be tracked and addressed, not concealed to make a work queue look complete.

## Education and Training

NCRA publishes several routes to eligibility for the Oncology Data Specialist examination. One route combines an NCRA-accredited associate degree program with its required practicum activities and competency assessments. Another combines an associate degree or equivalent college credit with an NCRA-accredited certificate program and its practicum requirements.

An experience-based route combines specified college education, anatomy and physiology coursework, and qualifying cancer-registry experience. Because these routes combine education with demonstrated practice, a general medical coding course alone should not be assumed to meet ODS eligibility.

High-school biology, anatomy, mathematics, computing, and careful written work provide useful preparation. NCI's free SEER training modules offer an introduction to cancer registration. They can help students explore the work, but reading modules alone is not the same as completing an accredited program, practicum, or certification requirements.

## Certification and Licensure

The **Oncology Data Specialist (ODS)** credential is administered through the National Cancer Registrars Association's Council on Certification. Candidates must satisfy an eligibility route and pass the examination. Maintaining the credential requires continuing education and compliance with NCRA's current maintenance requirements.

ODS is the new name for the **Certified Tumor Registrar (CTR)** credential; implementation of the name change began in January 2024. It is a credential name, while cancer registrar remains an occupational title. Older references to CTR may therefore refer to the same credential under its former name.

Certification concerns competence in cancer registration. It is not a medical license and does not authorize cancer diagnosis or treatment. Employers may require ODS certification; students should compare the requirement in a particular vacancy with the published eligibility pathway before choosing training.

## Salary, Entry-Level Earnings, and Job Outlook

BLS includes cancer registrars within **Health Information Technologists and Medical Registrars**. For that combined U.S. occupational group, the **median annual wage was $68,020 in May 2025**, with **16% projected employment growth from 2025 to 2035** and approximately **3,000 openings per year**, on average.

These are not cancer-registrar-only statistics. The group also includes health information technologists and other medical registrar work. Its median is a midpoint across workers, not a starting salary. For an entry position, examine the employer's posted range, credential expectations, and whether the duties involve hospital abstraction, central-registry work, or another specialty.

## Specialties and Career Advancement

Registrars can develop expertise in hospital cancer programs, population-based surveillance, particular cancer sites, data quality, or registry operations. Some become the colleague others consult about difficult abstractions or changing reporting requirements. Others focus on training, auditing, analysis, or coordinating a registry team.

Further education and experience can support management or research-related responsibilities. These are not automatic promotions from certification alone. A registrar moving into population-level analysis, for example, may need stronger statistical skills, while a supervisor needs to guide staff and maintain dependable processes. Continued learning is part of the occupation because definitions, treatments, and reporting standards change.

## Benefits and Challenges of the Career

Cancer registration offers a way to contribute to cancer prevention and care through information. A carefully resolved record can improve the data used to understand outcomes and identify community needs. The work may appeal to someone who enjoys medical science and detailed investigation without wanting a bedside role.

Its challenges include complex rules, incomplete source documents, reporting deadlines, and the emotional weight of cancer histories. Results are often indirect: the registrar may never meet the people whose information contributes to a study or program. Satisfaction can come from knowing that the evidence others use is more dependable because of careful work.

## Career Connections and Related Occupations

A **clinical coder** translates documented diagnoses and services into codes for purposes that include reimbursement. A **cancer registrar** assembles a cancer-specific history across diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up using registry standards. Both classify information, but their records and purposes differ.

A **health information technologist** may work on systems and data analysis across many clinical services. An **epidemiologist** studies patterns and causes of disease in populations and may use registry data. An **oncologist** is a physician treating cancer. Registrars collaborate with clinical and research professionals while maintaining a distinct responsibility for the quality of cancer information.

## Could This Career Interest You?

Would you enjoy piecing together information from several documents and explaining why two entries do not agree? Are you interested in cancer biology and public health, as well as detailed records work?

Explore an introductory SEER module and notice whether the process of turning a medical story into consistent data interests you. When talking with a registrar, ask how hospital and central-registry work differ, how uncertain cases are resolved, and how new staff learn the reporting rules. Those questions reveal the investigative side of the occupation.

## Professional Associations

The **National Cancer Registrars Association (NCRA)** is the professional association for cancer registry practitioners. It provides education, advocacy, professional resources, and the ODS credential through its Council on Certification. Its materials include career information and routes into the profession.

[NCRA's introduction to the profession](https://www.ncra-usa.org/About/Cancer-Registry-Profession) is a useful starting point. NCI's [SEER training resources](https://seer.cancer.gov/training/) provide additional learning opportunities; SEER is a federal cancer-surveillance program, not a membership association.
