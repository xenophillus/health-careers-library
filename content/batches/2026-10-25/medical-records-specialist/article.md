## Career Overview

Medical records technicians help turn a collection of clinical notes, test results, and treatment information into an accurate, usable patient record. Their work supports continuing care, appropriate access to information, billing, and the analysis of healthcare services. They usually contribute through records and computer systems rather than hands-on treatment.

The occupation includes different assignments. One technician may concentrate on finding missing documentation; another may handle authorized record requests. Medical coding is a related specialization that translates documented diagnoses and services into standardized codes. A job title alone does not tell you which of these responsibilities a position includes.

## Duties and Responsibilities

The technician's responsibility is to preserve the quality and appropriate availability of the record. Major areas of work include:

- Record quality: Check that documents are complete, correctly associated with a patient, and entered in the appropriate place.
- Documentation follow-up: Contact the responsible clinical team when information is missing or needs clarification.
- Classification: In coding positions, assign codes supported by clinical documentation and the applicable coding rules.
- Retrieval and release: Locate records and follow the organization's process for providing information to authorized recipients.
- Information protection: Use access controls, secure communication, and records procedures that protect confidentiality.

A technician does not solve an unclear diagnosis by inventing one. Reliable records depend on distinguishing what the clinician documented from an assumption made while reading it.

## A Day in the Life

Consider an illustrative day in a hospital records department. The technician begins with a work queue of recently completed visits. One record has all the required documents; another is missing a report. The technician routes the missing-item request to the appropriate team and records the follow-up so another worker can see what remains unresolved.

Later, a request for records arrives. Before releasing information, the technician follows the department's verification and disclosure procedures. In a coding assignment, the afternoon might instead involve reviewing documentation, choosing supported codes, and discussing a difficult case with a more experienced coder. These examples represent different assignments, not tasks every technician performs each day.

## Work Environment and Schedule

Hospitals and physician practices are important employers, but health information work also occurs in organizations that support healthcare operations. Much of the day involves computer-based work, with communication by telephone, secure messages, or meetings.

Most positions are full time. A department supporting a hospital that operates around the clock may need evening or weekend coverage. The work is less physically active than bedside care, but it is not solitary: unclear records often require communication with clinicians, coding colleagues, privacy staff, and billing teams.

## Skills, Knowledge, and Personal Qualities

Medical terminology helps a technician understand the record without confusing similar words or abbreviations. Knowledge of anatomy and healthcare documentation supports accurate reading, while coding positions also require skill with classification rules. Computer fluency matters because finding information, tracking unresolved items, and entering data are central parts of the job.

Careful reading and clear questions are equally important. “This report is absent” is more useful than a vague message that a chart is wrong. Integrity means resisting pressure to fill gaps with guesses or to access a record out of curiosity. A person's medical history remains private even when the technician recognizes the patient's name.

## Physical and Emotional Demands

Sustained screen work and repeated keyboard or mouse use are practical demands of the occupation. A workable desk arrangement, changes in posture, and appropriate breaks support comfort during detailed computer tasks.

The emotional demand is often responsibility for information rather than exposure to a medical emergency. A small error can affect a patient's record, an insurance claim, or a colleague's work. Sensitive histories also require discretion. A technician needs to maintain accuracy when the work queue grows and to ask for help when a record or request raises an unfamiliar issue.

## Education and Training

BLS identifies a postsecondary certificate as the typical entry qualification for medical records specialists, although employers may accept other routes or require a degree. Coding education and broader health information management education serve related but different goals.

For a student interested in the Registered Health Information Technician credential, the U.S. educational route is an associate-level health information management program accredited by CAHIIM. A short coding course is not automatically equivalent to that program. Conversely, someone preparing for an entry coding credential should examine its specific eligibility rules rather than assume a degree is always required.

In high school, practice careful reading, biology, written communication, and spreadsheets. A useful exploration activity is organizing a fictional set of visit documents and identifying missing information; real patient records should not be used for a school exercise.

## Certification and Licensure

Professional credentials demonstrate knowledge in a particular part of health information work. They are distinct from a license to diagnose or treat patients.

AHIMA's Registered Health Information Technician (RHIT) examination has an accredited health information management education route, as well as a specified international reciprocity route. AHIMA explicitly distinguishes eligible HIM programs from health informatics programs, which it does not currently accept for the RHIT exam.

AHIMA's Certified Coding Associate (CCA) exam requires a high-school diploma. Coding experience or relevant training is recommended rather than listed as a mandatory eligibility condition. Passing an exam and meeting the credential's other requirements are separate from being hired: employers may require particular credentials or experience. Both credentials have continuing-certification requirements.

## Salary, Entry-Level Earnings, and Job Outlook

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics reports a **May 2025 national median annual wage of $51,140 for Medical Records Specialists**, the occupational group that includes medical records technicians and medical coders. The median is the midpoint of reported wages, not an estimate of what a new graduate will earn.

BLS projects **8% employment growth from 2025 to 2035** and about **14,000 openings per year**, on average, for that group. Openings include replacement needs as well as growth. These figures are not separate wage or hiring estimates for every records-department job title. An actual entry offer depends on the job's responsibilities, location, employer, and required experience.

## Specialties and Career Advancement

A technician can develop expertise in record completeness, release of information, coding, or records quality. Coding itself includes different clinical settings and classification demands. Moving from one assignment to another may require additional training rather than simply a new title.

With experience and further education, workers may pursue auditing, team leadership, or broader health information management responsibilities. AHIMA offers credentials in areas such as coding, privacy, and analytics, but each has its own requirements. Advancement is most meaningful when it corresponds to greater responsibility and demonstrated expertise, not just a longer list of initials.

## Benefits and Challenges of the Career

This career offers a way to contribute to healthcare through careful investigation and organization. Someone who enjoys finding an inconsistency, understanding why it matters, and getting it resolved may find record-quality work satisfying. The contribution can be important even when the worker never meets the patient.

The tradeoff is that much of the work requires sustained attention to detailed information. Repetitive queues, deadlines, and changes in documentation or coding rules can make the job demanding. Someone seeking frequent hands-on patient care may prefer a different role; someone who enjoys information work should still expect collaboration and accountability.

## Career Connections and Related Occupations

A clinical coder specializes in translating documented care into classification codes; a medical records technician may have a wider records assignment or a position that includes coding. Health information technician is another title employers use, and the actual duties deserve close comparison.

A health information technologist focuses more on information systems, data analysis, and improving how information supports care. A cancer registrar builds specialized information about cancer cases and outcomes. A medical assistant usually supports clinic operations and may perform permitted clinical tasks. These roles can work with the same patient information while having different preparation and responsibilities.

## Could This Career Interest You?

Would you enjoy locating a missing piece of information and following the issue through to resolution? Do you like organized systems, careful reading, and work where accuracy matters more than being the fastest person in the room?

Compare a record-quality job description with a coding job description. Notice which mentions clinical terminology, coding credentials, records requests, or communication with providers. Ask an instructor or health information professional how a new employee's work is checked and how difficult cases are handled. Those details reveal more about the job than the title alone.

## Professional Associations

The **American Health Information Management Association (AHIMA)** supports health information professionals through education, credentials, professional resources, and advocacy. Its career materials cover the intersection of healthcare, information systems, and business. Its ethics resources explain why information integrity and confidentiality are professional responsibilities.

Explore [AHIMA's health information career introduction](https://www.ahima.org/certification-careers/certifications-overview/career-tools/career-pages/health-information-101/) and [professional association information](https://www.ahima.org/who-we-are/about-us/). Membership, earning a credential, and meeting an employer's hiring requirements are separate matters.
