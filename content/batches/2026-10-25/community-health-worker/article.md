## Career Overview

A community health worker (CHW) helps people connect health information with the realities of everyday life. A clinic may offer an appointment, but a person may still need transportation, help understanding the process, or a trusted person to discuss concerns with. CHWs work with individuals and communities to address such barriers and make services more accessible.

Their distinctive strength is a close understanding of the community they serve. This may come from shared language, culture, neighborhood ties or life experience. A CHW learns what matters to people, helps them navigate services, and brings community perspectives back to organizations. The relationship works in both directions: people learn about available resources, and organizations learn why those resources may not be reaching everyone.

Community health work combines education, practical problem-solving, advocacy and relationship-building. It is a public-health role with many possible job titles and settings; it does not make someone a physician, nurse or other independently licensed clinician.

## Duties and Responsibilities

The work centers on helping people act on their own health priorities and helping services respond to community needs.

- **Outreach and trust:** Meet residents, explain available support, and develop relationships with people who may not be reached by conventional clinic communication.
- **Navigation and follow-up:** Help someone understand a referral, identify transportation or other resources, and check whether the connection actually worked.
- **Health education and coaching:** Explain reliable information in an understandable, culturally appropriate way and support goals a person chooses.
- **Advocacy:** Communicate barriers and community priorities to service providers, agencies or local organizations.
- **Records and program improvement:** Record contacts and referrals, gather feedback, and help a team understand which needs remain unmet.

A referral is more than handing out a telephone number. A CHW may discover that a resource has a waiting list, requires documents a person does not have, or operates during the person’s work shift. Identifying the barrier allows the team to search for a realistic next step. Clinical tasks, when included in a particular position, depend on separate training, authorization and program rules.

## A Day in the Life

An illustrative day at a community organization begins with follow-up calls. A resident has not attended an appointment; instead of assuming a lack of interest, the CHW asks what happened. The resident describes a transportation problem. With permission, the worker helps explore available options and clarifies the appointment instructions.

Later, the CHW attends a neighborhood event. Conversations reveal that several families are confused about where to seek affordable services. The worker shares information, listens to concerns, and notes recurring questions for the team. In the afternoon, a scheduled visit focuses on a resident’s own goal, such as arranging a follow-up visit or finding a food resource. Before finishing, the worker documents agreed next steps and sends a question to the appropriate team member.

Another position might focus on youth mentoring, recovery support or outreach around a particular health condition. The common thread is continued contact and practical follow-through, rather than a fixed sequence of medical procedures.

## Work Environment and Schedule

CHWs work in healthcare organizations, public agencies and community-based organizations. Work can alternate between an office, neighborhood events, community spaces and home visits. Telephone and online contact may help maintain connections between visits. Some roles involve substantial travel within a service area; others are based in a clinic or program office.

Most positions are full time, and evening or weekend events can be part of the schedule. A community meeting held after work may be more accessible to residents than an appointment during business hours. Teams may include clinicians, social-service staff and other outreach workers, but a CHW also spends time building relationships outside the employing organization.

The setting changes the practical demands. A clinic-based worker may coordinate closely with a care team, while a neighborhood outreach worker may spend more time arranging meetings and connecting different agencies.

## Skills, Knowledge, and Personal Qualities

Listening is a working skill in this occupation. A person’s first request may be for an appointment, while further conversation reveals fear, cost or a previous bad experience. Effective CHWs ask clear questions, avoid assumptions and help people describe what they want to change.

Local knowledge matters: which services exist, whom they serve, how people contact them, and what obstacles can arise. The worker also needs reliable documentation and follow-up habits. A clear record helps coworkers understand what was offered, what the person agreed to, and what still needs attention.

Cultural responsiveness means adapting communication while respecting the individual, rather than assuming that everyone from the same background has the same beliefs. Language skills can help, but knowing another language is not the same as being qualified for every professional interpretation task. Professional judgment includes recognizing when to involve someone with different expertise.

## Physical and Emotional Demands

Physical demands depend on the assignment. Community visits may involve walking, travel, standing at events and carrying educational materials, while office work involves screens and documentation. A worker planning visits needs to understand the organization’s safety, communication and confidentiality procedures for work away from the office.

The emotional demands can be considerable. A CHW may hear about housing instability, violence, illness or discrimination and may share some of those experiences. Supporting a person through a slow or unsuccessful referral can be frustrating. In a BLS career interview, CHW Mikayla Trujillo described the importance of boundaries when working with people in crisis.

Supportive supervision and opportunities to discuss difficult situations matter. Caring about a community does not mean being personally available at every hour or being able to solve every shortage of services. Clear responsibilities help a worker remain dependable without taking over the person’s decisions.

## Education and Training

Typical entry preparation is a high school diploma, with some employers seeking additional education. Training introduces the specific services, outreach methods, ethical responsibilities and documentation used in the job. Community experience also matters because the role depends on trust and knowledge of local circumstances.

In Kansas, the CHW Coalition describes a core-competency education program with **100 hours of instruction and 60 hours of service learning**. Service learning connects classroom topics with supervised community experience. Programs can differ in how those hours are organized, so applicants should check that the particular course is an approved route for the credential they intend to seek.

High-school students can begin developing relevant skills through communication courses, learning another language, studying public health or social issues, and participating in appropriately supervised community activities. Useful preparation includes listening without judgment and learning how to explain a process clearly. Volunteering is an opportunity to learn about a community, not a substitute for the training required by a specific employer or credential.

## Certification and Licensure

CHW certification rules are state-specific. A course completion certificate shows that someone finished a course; a professional certification is a separate credential with its own application requirements.

**Kansas:** KDHE lists education, work-experience and reciprocity pathways. The education route uses a KDHE-approved CHW program. The work-experience route requires **800 hours over three years and three recommendation letters** documenting work or volunteer experience. The reciprocity route evaluates qualifying credentials and training from elsewhere under Kansas criteria; it does not mean every out-of-state credential transfers automatically.

Kansas Medicaid’s CHW policy requires a KDHE-recognized certification for CHW services covered under that policy and places those services under an approved supervising practitioner. Those payment-program requirements should not be confused with a universal description of every community-based CHW job.

A CHW credential does not by itself authorize diagnosis, prescribing or independent clinical treatment. The job’s responsibilities, supervision and any additional qualifications need to match the services being provided. The Kansas Coalition maintains certification information and a registry for checking status.

## Salary, Entry-Level Earnings, and Job Outlook

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics reports a **median annual wage of $51,850 for community health workers in May 2025**. Half earned more and half earned less. The lowest-paid 10% earned below $38,810, and the highest-paid 10% earned above $77,180. These are national occupation-wide wage percentiles, not verified starting and experienced-worker salaries.

BLS projects **13% employment growth from 2025 to 2035**, with approximately **7,000 openings per year**. Openings include replacement needs as well as growth. The figures describe the occupation nationally and do not guarantee an opening in a particular community.

When comparing an entry-level position, look at the actual offered wage, hours, benefits, travel expectations and reimbursement arrangements. The national median cannot establish a new worker’s wage or a Kansas-specific rate.

## Specialties and Career Advancement

CHWs may develop expertise in a population, community or program, such as supporting people managing chronic conditions, connecting families with services, or working with residents affected by housing instability. A specialty changes the resources and relationships the worker needs to understand; it does not automatically expand clinical authority.

Community Health Representatives have a particular place in Tribal health systems. The Indian Health Service describes CHRs as a Tribal community health workforce supporting access and community capacity in American Indian and Alaska Native communities. Promotores and promotoras are also part of the broader CHW field. These titles carry community and program context, so they should not be treated as identical credentials in every setting.

Experienced workers may become team leads, supervisors, trainers or advocates for the workforce. Further study can lead toward health education, public health or a separately regulated profession. Becoming a nurse, social worker or counselor requires that profession’s own education and credential pathway; CHW experience can inform the transition but does not replace it.

## Benefits and Challenges of the Career

One reward is seeing a practical barrier become manageable: someone connects with a service, gains confidence asking questions, or returns to pursue a goal after an unsuccessful attempt. Community knowledge and lived experience can become professional strengths. The work also offers opportunities to make organizations more responsive to the people they serve.

The challenges often come from conditions outside the worker’s direct control. A helpful referral cannot create housing where there is none, extend a program’s eligibility or eliminate a long waiting list. Success may involve small steps and repeated contact rather than a quick resolution.

Trust also creates responsibility. A worker may be known personally in the community and professionally by an employer. Respecting confidentiality, communicating boundaries and avoiding promises the program cannot keep are central to preserving that trust. Someone who values sustained relationships may find this meaningful; someone seeking immediate, easily measured results may find parts of it difficult.

## Career Connections and Related Occupations

**Health education specialists** design and evaluate educational programs and materials. Their typical entry education is at least a bachelor’s degree. CHWs also educate, but their distinctive contribution is a trusted community connection, outreach and practical navigation. The two roles may collaborate on the same program.

**Social workers** help people address broader social and personal difficulties; clinical social workers can diagnose and treat mental and behavioral conditions under their professional qualifications. CHWs connect people with such services and provide support within their own role rather than acquiring clinical authority through a CHW credential.

**Patient navigator** is a job title that may overlap with CHW work. Navigation describes a function—helping someone move through services—while CHW describes a broader community-centered role. A position’s duties and required qualifications are more informative than the title alone. Some navigation jobs have clinical responsibilities or require a separate healthcare credential, so an applicant should read the position carefully.

## Could This Career Interest You?

Do you enjoy helping someone understand an unfamiliar system without taking over their choices? Are you interested in why a service that looks available on paper may still be difficult to use? Can you listen to criticism of an organization and bring the concern back constructively?

Imagine that you provide a referral and later learn the person could not use it. Would you want to understand the obstacle and follow up? Imagine a neighborhood group identifies a problem your employer had not noticed. Would you be comfortable helping the group explain its concerns?

This career may appeal to someone who values community relationships, practical problem-solving and patient progress. Exploring a supervised volunteer role or speaking with a CHW program can help you learn whether the mix of outreach, records and follow-through suits you.

## Professional Associations

The **National Association of Community Health Workers (NACHW)** is a membership organization that brings CHWs and allies together around professional development, advocacy and the role of community health workers in health equity. Its resources help explain the workforce’s identity and the importance of community leadership.

The **Kansas Community Health Worker Coalition** provides state-level education, certification resources, connections and advocacy. Its information is particularly useful for understanding Kansas preparation routes and professional opportunities.

The **National Council on CHW Core Consensus Standards**, often called the C3 Council, develops consensus descriptions of CHW roles and skills. These provide a way to understand the profession’s breadth; they are not a license or a substitute for an employer’s job description.
