## Career Overview

Occupational therapists (OTs) help people participate in meaningful everyday activities. Here, “occupation” includes self-care, school, play, family responsibilities, work, and leisure—not only paid employment. Illness, injury, disability, or developmental needs can make these activities difficult.

An OT considers the person, the activity, and the environment together. Improving a physical or cognitive skill may help; changing how a task is performed or adapting the surroundings may help too. The aim is participation in the person’s life, rather than improvement on an isolated test without a useful daily purpose.

## Duties and Responsibilities

Occupational therapy brings several responsibilities together:

- **Understand priorities:** Learn what the client needs or wants to do and what currently gets in the way.
- **Evaluate performance:** Observe relevant activities and assess physical, cognitive, and other factors affecting them.
- **Plan and provide intervention:** Select meaningful activities, practice strategies, or adaptations suited to the person’s goals.
- **Adapt tasks and environments:** Recommend or teach use of equipment and changes that support participation and safety.
- **Work with others:** Train caregivers, collaborate with the team, document outcomes, and adjust the plan.

The same difficulty can call for different solutions for different people. The therapist needs to understand what an activity means in that person’s actual routine.

## A Day in the Life

Imagine an OT in a rehabilitation service working with someone who wants to manage a morning routine after an illness. The therapist asks which parts are difficult and observes the person performing relevant activities. Together they choose a manageable goal and practice a strategy or adaptation.

Later, the therapist discusses how a caregiver can support the plan without taking over everything the person can do. Documentation records not just that an activity occurred, but what assistance or strategy made a difference. The next session builds on that response.

This is an illustrative workflow. A school-based OT might instead collaborate with a teacher about classroom participation, while another OT helps a client build routines that support mental health.

## Work Environment and Schedule

OTs work in hospitals, therapy offices, nursing facilities, home health services, and educational settings. Full-time work is common, and some schedules include evenings or weekends to fit clients’ needs.

The setting shapes the activities available for assessment and practice. A clinic may use a simulated daily task; a home visit can reveal barriers in the actual environment. School-based work involves collaboration with educators and participation in the educational program. Occupational therapy also has a role in mental health, where routines, roles, and engagement in daily life can be central concerns.

## Skills, Knowledge, and Personal Qualities

An OT needs scientific knowledge and the ability to analyze an activity carefully. An everyday task can involve movement, attention, planning, coordination, and features of the environment. Identifying which part is difficult is more useful than assuming the whole activity is beyond the person.

Creativity must be grounded in assessment and evidence. A clever adaptation is useful only if it fits the client’s goals and circumstances. Listening, clear explanations, patience, and accurate documentation support this process. Respecting the person’s choices is particularly important when working on private routines such as dressing or personal care.

## Physical and Emotional Demands

Depending on the setting, OTs may stand, bend, work at a child’s level, handle equipment, or assist someone during a daily activity. Safe handling practices and appropriate assistance matter when mobility is limited; NIOSH identifies patient handling as a significant source of worker injury.

Clients may be frustrated by difficulty doing something that once felt ordinary. The therapist must take that experience seriously while helping identify achievable next steps. Progress may mean using an effective adaptation or receiving an appropriate level of support, rather than performing every activity without assistance. Close work with families and care teams can be emotionally demanding as well as rewarding.

## Education and Training

Entry-level occupational therapist education in the United States is available at the **master’s or doctoral level**. AOTA states that both routes remain available; a doctorate is not the only entry option. Programs include professional coursework and supervised fieldwork.

For U.S.-educated applicants, NBCOT exam eligibility requires graduation from an ACOTE-accredited occupational therapy program. An occupational therapy assistant program prepares for a separate role and is not interchangeable with an OT program. Also distinguish an entry-level degree from a postprofessional degree designed for someone already qualified in the profession.

Students should review the specific program’s prerequisites, degree structure, fieldwork arrangements, and accreditation status. Biology, psychology, and communication are useful foundations for a profession that connects people’s abilities with everyday activities and environments.

## Certification and Licensure

Education, national certification, and state licensure are separate parts of becoming an OT. NBCOT administers the Occupational Therapist Registered (OTR) certification examination. For U.S. graduates, eligibility requires the appropriate degree from an ACOTE-accredited OT program.

Kansas occupational therapists are regulated by the State Board of Healing Arts. K.S.A. 65-5406 requires an application supported by qualifying education, supervised fieldwork, examination, and applicable fees. K.S.A. 65-5407 addresses the required examination. The Board determines licensing qualifications; national certification is not a substitute for state authorization.

Kansas defines occupational therapy around meaningful activities, participation, and adaptation. Its statute expressly distinguishes occupational therapy from making a medical diagnosis. Clinical responsibilities must remain within professional preparation and applicable practice requirements.

## Salary, Entry-Level Earnings, and Job Outlook

The BLS **May 2025 U.S. median annual wage for occupational therapists was $100,330**. This is the midpoint across workers in the occupation, not verified starting pay or a Kansas wage estimate.

BLS projects **15% employment growth during 2025–2035** and approximately **10,000 openings per year**, on average. Openings include replacement needs as well as growth. These figures apply to occupational therapists, not occupational therapy assistants.

Pay differs across settings and locations. Comparing offers requires considering the actual role and schedule as well as the headline annual figure.

## Specialties and Career Advancement

OT practice can focus on children and youth, older adults, mental health, rehabilitation, or particular functional needs. A school-based therapist supports access to education; an OT working with older adults may focus on participation and safety in daily routines. Mental health practice can address the routines and roles that support well-being.

With further experience and learning, therapists may develop advanced clinical expertise, supervise services, teach, or participate in research. A specialty is not simply a different activity kit: it requires understanding the population and evidence relevant to its needs. Advanced credentials have their own eligibility requirements.

## Benefits and Challenges of the Career

The work can make an immediate difference in something personally important: preparing a meal, participating in class, returning to a hobby, or managing a routine. There is room for practical problem-solving alongside clinical knowledge. Solutions may involve the environment and other people as well as the client’s skills.

Challenges include emotionally difficult changes in a person’s life, slow progress, competing priorities, and coordinating a plan across settings. An adaptation that works in a therapy room may need revision at home or school. The therapist must balance creativity with evidence, feasibility, and respect for what the client wants.

## Career Connections and Related Occupations

**Occupational therapy assistants** participate in providing OT services under an occupational therapist’s supervision and have a separate education and credential route. **Physical therapists** focus on movement and physical function; both professions can contribute to rehabilitation and daily participation. **Speech-language pathologists** address communication and swallowing needs within their own scope.

An OT may also collaborate with teachers, nurses, physicians, social workers, and family caregivers. A classroom goal, for example, may require both an activity adaptation and collaboration with the teacher. OT is broader than job placement and is not limited to upper-body exercise.

## Could This Career Interest You?

Do you notice how the design of a task or space affects what someone can do? Would you enjoy learning what matters to a person and testing practical ways to support it? Can you be patient when a familiar task takes another person extra time or assistance?

Compare OT in a school, a rehabilitation service, and a mental health setting. Ask how the therapist chooses activities and measures whether they help outside the session. This can show whether you are drawn to the profession’s combination of science, daily-life problem-solving, and collaboration.

## Professional Associations

The [American Occupational Therapy Association (AOTA)](https://www.aota.org/about) offers professional education, practice resources, publications, advocacy, and communities for occupational therapy practitioners. Its public explanation of OT and school directory can help students explore the field.

AOTA is distinct from ACOTE, which accredits education programs; NBCOT, which provides national certification; and the state board, which regulates practice. Understanding these roles helps students identify the right source for career information, school accreditation, exam eligibility, or legal requirements.
