## Career Overview

Athletic trainers are healthcare professionals who help prevent, evaluate, and treat injuries and illnesses related to physical activity. They provide emergency care, guide rehabilitation, and support a safe return to activity. Although sports are a familiar setting, athletic trainers also work with active people of different ages, performing artists, and people in occupational or tactical settings.

Athletic training is a healthcare profession with graduate-level entry education, national certification, and state practice requirements. Athletic trainers work in collaboration with physicians and other healthcare professionals. Athletic trainers are different from personal trainers and strength coaches, whose primary focus is exercise instruction or performance conditioning.

## Duties and Responsibilities

An athletic trainer’s work spans prevention, emergency response, treatment, and recovery. Core responsibilities include:

- Injury and illness prevention: Identify risk factors, develop prevention programs, recommend protective equipment, and educate patients about safe physical activity.
- Clinical evaluation and diagnosis: Examine injuries and illnesses, assess symptoms and physical function, make clinical judgments within their scope of practice, and determine when referral is necessary.
- Immediate and emergency care: Recognize and respond to serious injuries and medical emergencies, provide appropriate immediate care, and activate emergency response procedures when needed.
- Treatment and rehabilitation: Develop and carry out treatment plans, use therapeutic exercises and other appropriate interventions, and monitor progress during recovery.
- Return to activity: Evaluate recovery and functional readiness, guide reconditioning, and coordinate a safe return to sports, work, or other physical activities.
- Patient education and healthcare coordination: Explain injuries, prevention strategies, treatment plans, and activity restrictions; collaborate with physicians and other healthcare professionals.
- Documentation and administration: Maintain patient records, document evaluations and treatments, manage supplies and equipment, and help maintain emergency and safety procedures.

Athletic trainers work with patients of different ages and activity levels. Their specific responsibilities depend on their workplace, professional preparation, and applicable state laws.

## A Day in the Life

Imagine an athletic trainer working at a high school. Before practices begin, the athletic trainer checks supplies and emergency equipment, follows up with students recovering from injuries, and coordinates with coaches about activity restrictions. During the afternoon, the athletic trainer may evaluate a new complaint, guide rehabilitation sessions, and prepare taping or other protective supports.

During practice, a new injury can interrupt scheduled care. The athletic trainer assesses the student, provides immediate care, and decides whether another healthcare professional needs to evaluate the problem. For a student already recovering, the task may instead be to review progress and adjust rehabilitation with the care team. The work therefore combines quick decisions with follow-through over days or weeks.

After practice, records, cleaning, supply checks, and communication still need attention. This illustrative school workday differs from a clinic schedule built around appointments or a traveling team’s competition calendar.

## Work Environment and Schedule

Athletic trainers work in secondary schools, colleges, hospitals, physician offices, rehabilitation settings, sports organizations, and other workplaces. Some work mainly in a clinic or treatment room; others spend significant time on fields, sidelines, courts, or training facilities. Their patients are not limited to competitive athletes.

Most work full time. Athletic trainers covering sports practices and events may have evening, weekend, holiday, and travel commitments. Outdoor coverage can require working in heat, cold, or rain. A clinical position may offer more predictable hours, depending on the employer.

## Skills, Knowledge, and Personal Qualities

A solid foundation in anatomy, physiology, movement, injury assessment, and emergency response supports the profession. Athletic trainers also need careful observation, sound clinical judgment, accurate records, and the ability to explain instructions clearly.

Communication matters when an injured person is frightened, a parent needs an explanation, or a coach wants to know whether participation is safe. Athletic trainers need to remain composed under pressure, respect patient privacy, follow professional boundaries, and work effectively with other healthcare professionals. Being interested in sports can help, but enjoying science, healthcare, and patient interaction is equally important.

## Physical and Emotional Demands

The work can involve prolonged standing, walking between activity areas, kneeling beside a patient, and moving supplies or equipment. Some situations demand quick physical responses, especially when an injury occurs during an event. Workspaces may be noisy, crowded, or outdoors.

Emotionally, an athletic trainer may manage time pressure, a serious emergency, or disappointment when a patient cannot return to activity as quickly as hoped. Clear communication and teamwork are essential when patients, families, coaches, and clinicians have different concerns. The specific demands vary widely between a sports sideline and a medical clinic.

## Education and Training

A common educational pathway is:

1. Complete high school, building a foundation in biology, anatomy and physiology, mathematics, and communication.
2. Earn a bachelor’s degree and complete the prerequisites for an accredited professional athletic training program. The undergraduate major and prerequisite courses depend on the program.
3. Complete a professional master’s degree in athletic training accredited by the Commission on Accreditation of Athletic Training Education (CAATE). Professional education combines classroom study with supervised clinical experience.
4. Meet BOC certification requirements and the practice requirements of the state where you intend to work.

A traditional four-year bachelor’s degree followed by a two-year professional master’s takes about six years after high school. Some combined programs award both degrees in five years. For example, the University of New England offers a two-year master’s route and an integrated 3+2 bachelor’s/master’s route. These are examples of program design, not a single timetable that every school follows.

For students entering the profession now, CAATE identifies graduate education as the entry route. An exercise science degree or personal-training certificate alone is not an athletic training professional qualification. Clinical placements matter because students must apply their knowledge to patients and learn to communicate decisions as members of a healthcare team.

## Certification and Licensure

For the U.S. accredited-program route, Board of Certification (BOC) certification requires completing a CAATE-accredited professional athletic training program, passing the BOC examination, and submitting the required documentation, including a final official transcript and current emergency cardiac care certification. Maintaining certification involves continuing education, emergency cardiac care certification, and other BOC renewal requirements.

**Kansas:** Athletic trainers establishing practice in Kansas must obtain a license from the Kansas State Board of Healing Arts. Each licensed athletic trainer must file a practice protocol with the Board. A separate protocol is required for each responsible MD, DO, or DC who delegates duties; both that clinician and the athletic trainer sign it. The protocol identifies the delegated responsibilities.

BOC certification demonstrates professional qualification; the state license and practice framework govern legal practice. Earning the national credential does not replace the Kansas licensing process.

## Salary, Entry-Level Earnings, and Job Outlook

According to the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), the **national median annual wage for athletic trainers was $62,520 in May 2025**. This means half of athletic trainers earned more than that amount and half earned less. The lowest-paid 10% earned less than **$48,180**, while the highest-paid 10% earned more than **$88,760**. These figures describe workers across all experience levels; they are **not starting-salary estimates**.

BLS projects athletic trainer employment to grow **13% between 2025 and 2035**, faster than the 3% growth projected for all occupations. It estimates approximately **2,400 job openings per year**, on average, over that decade. Openings include both newly created positions and jobs that become available when workers leave the occupation.

Actual pay depends on location, employer, practice setting, and experience. Students researching a first job should examine recent local postings and state wage data alongside the national figures.

Source: [BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook — Athletic Trainers](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/athletic-trainers.htm).

## Specialties and Career Advancement

An athletic trainer might specialize through experience and continuing education in a particular environment or patient population, such as secondary school athletics, college sports, professional sports, physician practices, occupational settings, performing arts, or tactical populations. Some work closely with orthopedic and sports medicine teams.

With experience, athletic trainers may move into lead or head athletic trainer positions, management, athletic administration, education, or clinical program coordination. Certain advanced roles call for additional education, credentials, or experience. Career advancement can mean developing expertise and responsibilities rather than working for a famous sports team.

## Benefits and Challenges of the Career

The appeal of athletic training often comes from building relationships with patients, seeing recovery progress, combining medical knowledge with movement, and being present during important moments of activity and competition. The work can be varied and meaningful.

Challenges can include demanding schedules, evening or weekend events, travel, physical fatigue, and responsibility during emergencies. Patients may face long recoveries, and athletic trainers must communicate decisions even when an athlete or coach is disappointed. Graduate education and credential maintenance also require time and financial investment. Benefits and working conditions depend on the employer.

## Career Connections and Related Occupations

Athletic trainers collaborate with physicians, physical therapists, occupational therapists, nurses, emergency medical services personnel, and other professionals. Their work overlaps with several careers, but training pathways and legal responsibilities differ.

- **Physical therapist:** Focuses on movement, function, and rehabilitation across a wide range of patient needs; follows a different degree and licensing pathway.
- **Sports medicine physician:** A physician with specialized training who diagnoses and treats medical conditions and injuries.
- **Strength and conditioning coach:** Emphasizes physical performance and conditioning rather than the clinical scope of an athletic trainer.
- **Emergency medical technician:** Provides prehospital emergency care within a distinct certification and regulatory system.

Comparing these professions can help clarify whether a student is most drawn to injury care, rehabilitation, exercise performance, or emergency response.

## Could This Career Interest You?

Consider which parts of the job are most interesting to you:

- Do I enjoy learning how the human body moves and responds to injury?
- Would I be comfortable helping someone who is hurt, upset, or uncertain?
- Am I interested in a healthcare career that may involve sports, active workplaces, or rehabilitation?
- Could I handle a schedule that sometimes includes games, evenings, or weekends?
- Am I willing to complete a graduate-level professional program and maintain credentials?

If possible, compare an athletic trainer's work in a school with an athletic trainer working in a clinic. Seeing different settings can reveal what the occupation is really like.

## Professional Associations

The [National Athletic Trainers’ Association (NATA)](https://www.nata.org/about) is a professional membership organization for athletic trainers and others supporting the profession. It offers professional education, publications, advocacy, and opportunities to connect with colleagues in different practice settings.

Its career information is useful for exploring work beyond competitive sports, including performing arts, occupational health, and physician practices. NATA membership, BOC certification, and state licensure have different purposes: joining an association does not qualify someone to practice.
