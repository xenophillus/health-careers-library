# Occupational Therapy Assistant

## Career Overview

Occupational therapy assistants (OTAs) help people participate in the activities of everyday life. In this field, an “occupation” can be dressing, learning, preparing food, playing, or taking part in work and community life. The goal is meaningful participation, not simply completing an exercise.

OTAs provide occupational therapy under an occupational therapist's direction. They help carry out treatment and observe how the person responds. A successful session connects a therapeutic activity with something the client needs or wants to do beyond the therapy setting.

## Duties and Responsibilities

An OTA guides clients through activities in a treatment plan, teaches ways to use adaptive equipment, and records progress. The assistant communicates observations to the occupational therapist so the team can consider whether the approach is helping.

For example, practicing a daily task can reveal more than whether it was completed. The client may need a reminder, a different arrangement of materials, or physical assistance. Recording that distinction helps the team understand participation and support needs. OTAs also teach family members or caregivers how to support agreed activities.

## A Day in the Life

Imagine an illustrative rehabilitation session with a client working toward dressing more independently after illness. Following the occupational therapist's plan, the OTA helps the person practice a strategy and observes where help is needed. The task has an everyday purpose: getting ready for the day with as much participation as possible.

After the session, the OTA records what happened and discusses relevant observations with the therapist. A later client may have a different goal involving school or a household routine. The activity changes because the goal and the person change; it is not simply a standard sequence repeated for everyone.

## Work Environment and Schedule

OTAs work in settings such as therapy practices, hospitals, nursing facilities, schools, and people's homes. A workplace influences both the clients served and the equipment available. Some positions involve travel or schedules arranged around client needs.

A school-based question might concern participation in a learning activity, while rehabilitation work may focus on returning to a daily routine. Ask professionals how they coordinate with the occupational therapist and other staff in each setting. The relationship between the session and the client's ordinary environment is especially important.

## Skills, Knowledge, and Personal Qualities

Observation and communication are essential. An OTA needs to explain an activity clearly, notice the client's response, and document information that another practitioner can use. Creativity is useful when adapting an activity within the plan, but it must stay connected to a therapeutic purpose.

Students can explore this way of thinking by breaking an ordinary task into steps. What skills, objects, and surroundings does it require? What would change if one step became difficult? This exercise introduces the profession's attention to the relationship between the person, activity, and environment.

## Physical and Emotional Demands

The work may involve standing, bending, and assisting clients with movement. Safe techniques and appropriate help matter, especially when a client needs physical support. Close contact also requires infection-control practices.

Progress may be gradual or uneven. A client can feel frustrated by an activity that used to be simple. Patience does not mean ignoring that frustration; it means responding respectfully while helping the person work toward a useful goal. Ask practitioners how they encourage participation without taking over a task the client is learning to do.

## Education and Training

AOTA identifies both associate-degree and bachelor's-degree entry routes for occupational therapy assistants. Students should look for an appropriately accredited OTA program and examine its supervised fieldwork arrangements. Accreditation is important for understanding eligibility for later certification and licensure.

Coursework and practice connect knowledge of people and health with therapeutic activities. A high-school student can build foundations in biology, psychology, communication, and careful observation. Compare the complete education pathway, including prerequisites and fieldwork, rather than assuming that every program has the same schedule or that a general rehabilitation certificate qualifies someone as an OTA.

## Certification and Licensure

The National Board for Certification in Occupational Therapy (NBCOT) awards the **Certified Occupational Therapy Assistant (COTA)** credential to eligible candidates who meet its requirements. NBCOT explicitly explains that certification is not the same as state licensure.

In Kansas, K.S.A. 65-5406 describes requirements involving an approved academic program, supervised fieldwork, and examination. K.S.A. 65-5402 defines the assistant's role as providing services under the direction of an occupational therapist. Students preparing to practice should follow current Kansas State Board of Healing Arts instructions for their license application, as well as NBCOT's certification requirements.

## Salary, Entry-Level Earnings, and Job Outlook

BLS reports a **national median annual wage of $72,300 for occupational therapy assistants in May 2025**. This is the assistant-specific figure, not the combined assistant-and-aide median. It is not a guaranteed starting wage or a Kansas-specific estimate.

BLS projects **21% employment growth for OTAs from 2025 to 2035**. The projection describes the occupation nationally, not an individual school's graduate placement rate. When comparing jobs, consider work setting, hours, travel, benefits, and opportunities for supervision and continued learning alongside the advertised wage.

## Specialties and Career Advancement

OTAs can deepen their experience with particular populations or practice areas, such as children, older adults, or physical rehabilitation. AOTA provides continuing-development resources and practice communities. Greater expertise can improve how an assistant delivers care within their role.

Becoming an occupational therapist is a separate educational and credentialing pathway; experience as an assistant does not automatically convert into therapist authorization. A student considering that later change should compare the additional degree and licensure requirements directly. Remaining an experienced OTA is also a meaningful career goal.

## Benefits and Challenges of the Career

An appealing feature is helping people do something that matters in ordinary life. A small improvement in participation may have significance that is not obvious to someone watching only the exercise or activity.

Challenges include physical work, documentation, and adapting to different needs without losing sight of the treatment goal. Progress cannot always be measured by speed or complete independence. Students interested in this career should consider whether they value careful, individualized work and can stay engaged when improvement takes time.

## Career Connections and Related Occupations

Occupational therapists and assistants work together but have different preparation and responsibilities. Occupational therapy aides usually perform support work and are not interchangeable with trained assistants. Physical therapist assistants work in another profession with its own education and scope.

The distinction becomes clearer when comparing the central question behind care. Occupational therapy asks how a person can participate in meaningful activities. The team may address movement, thinking, routines, equipment, or the environment as part of that question, often alongside other professionals.

## Could This Career Interest You?

Do you notice how a task could be made more accessible? Would you enjoy explaining, observing, and adjusting your communication for different people? Can you appreciate a person's effort without assuming that everyone should complete a task in the same way?

In an informational interview, ask an OTA how one treatment activity connects to a client's daily life and what observations they share with the therapist. Ask how their workplace differs from another setting. These questions reveal the reasoning behind the work as well as its visible activities.

## Professional Associations

The **American Occupational Therapy Association (AOTA)** provides information for students and practitioners, school-search resources, professional development, and materials about occupational therapy practice. Its explanations of everyday occupations are especially useful for understanding the profession's purpose.

NBCOT administers national certification, while the state board administers licensure. Knowing which organization handles which responsibility helps students use professional resources without confusing association membership, certification, and legal authorization.
