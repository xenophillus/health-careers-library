# Physical Therapy Assistant

## Career Overview

A physical therapist assistant, usually called a PTA, helps people improve movement and function through care directed by a physical therapist. The name “physical therapy assistant” is also used in career listings, but physical therapist assistant is the professional title. PTAs work with people recovering from injury or illness, managing pain or learning to move more safely.

A PTA is a clinician, not the same role as a physical therapy aide. The physical therapist evaluates the patient and establishes the plan of care; the PTA carries out assigned treatment, observes responses and communicates what happens. That teamwork connects hands-on practice with the patient's larger rehabilitation goals.

## Duties and Responsibilities

A PTA may guide therapeutic exercises, help a patient practice walking, teach use of mobility equipment and provide other treatments included in the physical therapist's plan. During a session, the assistant watches how the person moves and responds, offers instructions and records relevant observations. Progress notes help the therapist understand whether the treatment is producing the expected result.

The work requires more than counting repetitions. A patient may perform an exercise differently when tired or report a new concern. The PTA must recognize when to communicate with the therapist rather than simply continue. Treatment responsibilities remain within the assistant's preparation, delegated role and applicable practice requirements.

## A Day in the Life

Imagine a PTA in an outpatient rehabilitation clinic. Before the first appointment, the assistant reviews the physical therapist's plan and the previous session's notes. One patient practices a movement needed for getting up from a chair; another needs repeated instruction to use a walking device more safely. The PTA explains the purpose of the activity and observes how the person responds.

A patient reports a change that was not present at the last visit. The PTA brings it to the therapist's attention and follows the team's direction. Between appointments, documentation and preparation still need time. This illustrative day shows the combination of physical assistance, coaching, observation and communication.

## Work Environment and Schedule

PTAs work in outpatient practices, hospitals, nursing facilities, home health and other rehabilitation settings. In a clinic, patients may arrive for scheduled visits. In a hospital or residential setting, care must fit around other services and the person's changing condition. Home-based care adds travel and the need to work within an individual living environment.

Many work full time, although part-time positions exist. Evening or weekend work may help accommodate patients or facility schedules. The environment is usually active: assistants move between treatment areas and work closely with patients and other staff rather than spending the entire day at a desk.

## Skills, Knowledge, and Personal Qualities

Knowledge of anatomy, movement and rehabilitation helps a PTA understand why an activity is included in treatment. Observation helps identify a change in movement or tolerance. Accurate documentation gives the therapist useful information instead of a vague impression that a session went well.

Teaching is another major skill. A person may need a movement broken into smaller steps, a different explanation or time to practice. Patience does not mean ignoring difficulty; it means noticing what is preventing progress and communicating clearly. Professional boundaries, respect and dependable teamwork matter when people are frustrated or need physical assistance.

## Physical and Emotional Demands

PTAs spend substantial time on their feet and may assist patients with standing, walking or transfers. Safe techniques, equipment and help from colleagues are important when supporting someone whose balance or strength is limited. Repeated bending, reaching and close physical work can be tiring.

Rehabilitation also brings emotional demands. Progress may be slow, and a patient may be anxious about pain or discouraged by a loss of independence. The PTA needs to encourage effort without promising a particular recovery. Maintaining attention through repeated tasks is important because each patient's condition and response are different.

## Education and Training

A typical entry route is an associate degree from a physical therapist assistant program accredited by the Commission on Accreditation in Physical Therapy Education. These programs combine classroom and laboratory instruction with supervised clinical education. APTA describes PTA programs as typically taking about two years, although admission prerequisites and scheduling can affect an individual's total time.

Biology, anatomy, mathematics and communication are useful high-school preparation. Clinical placements are important because students must learn to apply techniques, interact with patients and recognize when to ask for help. A general exercise course or an aide job does not replace the professional PTA program and required practice authorization.

## Certification and Licensure

U.S. states regulate PTA practice, but their terminology differs. **Kansas calls the state authorization a physical therapist assistant certificate.** Under [K.S.A. 65-2906](https://www.kslegislature.gov/b2025_26/laws/065_000_0000_chapter/065_029_0000_article/065_029_0006_section/065_029_0006_k/), an applicant must meet the Board of Healing Arts' education and examination requirements, be of legal age and pay applicable fees. K.S.A. 65-2913 restricts use of the PTA title to appropriately authorized people, with provisions for compact privileges.

This state certificate is not the same as a school completion certificate or optional professional recognition. Graduation and passing an examination are steps toward authorization; applicants also complete the board's application process. The [Kansas board's PTA page](https://www.ksbha.ks.gov/professions/physical-therapy-assistant-p-t-a) provides the current profession-specific information.

## Salary, Entry-Level Earnings, and Job Outlook

BLS reports a **May 2025 U.S. median annual wage of $68,380 for physical therapist assistants**. Half of assistants in the measured occupation earned more and half earned less. This figure is not the lower-paid physical therapist aide statistic, and it is not a verified starting salary.

Employment of **physical therapist assistants specifically is projected to grow 23% from 2025 to 2035** in the BLS detailed table. The combined assistant-and-aide figure shown elsewhere on the same page is 17%, so the categories should not be confused. Location, employer, schedule and experience influence actual offers. For a first-job comparison, look for local PTA postings that clearly state the role and required authorization.

## Specialties and Career Advancement

PTAs can develop experience with particular patients or settings, such as older adults, postoperative rehabilitation, neurological conditions or home health. Additional education and supervised experience can deepen skills while the assistant continues to work within the PTA role.

Some move into staff education, coordination or leadership responsibilities. Becoming a physical therapist is a different educational and licensing path; years of assistant experience do not automatically convert a PTA into a PT. Comparing the two roles early helps students decide whether they prefer the assistant's treatment-focused work or the therapist's broader evaluation and planning responsibilities.

## Benefits and Challenges of the Career

A major benefit is participating directly in practical improvements: a person may become more confident moving around home or return to an important activity. Repeated visits allow a PTA to see change over time and build a working relationship with the patient.

Challenges include physical effort, busy appointment schedules and the need to stay patient when recovery is uneven. Documentation is part of care, even after a demanding session. A PTA must also accept the limits of the assistant role and communicate concerns clearly so the physical therapist can make the appropriate decisions.

## Career Connections and Related Occupations

A **physical therapist** evaluates movement problems and establishes the plan of care. A **physical therapy aide** generally supports operations with tasks such as preparing areas and assisting with nonclinical duties. A **PTA** provides treatment under the therapist's direction and supervision; these three titles are not interchangeable.

An **occupational therapy assistant** helps an occupational therapist support daily activities through a different professional pathway. An **exercise physiologist** focuses on exercise responses and programs. Comparing the purpose and supervision of these roles is more useful than assuming that all work involving exercise has the same qualifications.

## Could This Career Interest You?

Would you enjoy helping someone practice a movement many times while paying attention to small improvements? Could you explain an activity calmly when a person is frustrated? Are you comfortable with close physical work and the need to communicate changes to a supervising therapist?

Consider both the rewarding and repetitive parts of rehabilitation. A successful session may involve a modest gain rather than a dramatic recovery. Observing a PTA in more than one setting can show how patient needs change the daily work.

## Professional Associations

The [American Physical Therapy Association (APTA)](https://www.apta.org/) represents physical therapists, physical therapist assistants and students. Its career resources explain the PTA pathway, and its PTA community provides professional learning and connections.

Membership is separate from the state authorization required for practice. Students can use APTA's information to compare PTA education with PT and aide roles before choosing a program.
