# Physician (MD/DO)

## Career Overview

Physicians investigate health problems, diagnose disease, and guide treatment. In the United States, physicians may hold a Doctor of Medicine (MD) or Doctor of Osteopathic Medicine (DO) degree. Both routes lead to physician practice; specialty training shapes the kind of medicine a doctor eventually provides.

This is a broad career family. A family physician may care for patients over many years, while a surgeon concentrates on conditions requiring operations. Understanding the profession means exploring its different forms, rather than assuming every doctor has the same day.

## Duties and Responsibilities

Physicians combine a patient's account of symptoms with examination findings and other evidence. They decide which possibilities need investigation, explain treatment options, and monitor what happens next. They also document care and communicate with colleagues.

In family medicine, duties include prevention, management of ongoing illnesses, and referral when a problem needs another specialist. In other specialties, the central work may involve interpreting images, managing anesthesia, or performing operations. The shared responsibility is to use medical knowledge and judgment in the patient's care, within one's training and practice.

## A Day in the Life

Imagine an illustrative family-medicine day. A physician sees a patient for follow-up, reviews whether treatment is helping, and asks about new concerns. Another patient arrives with symptoms whose cause is unclear. Rather than naming a diagnosis immediately, the physician gathers evidence and explains what further evaluation may show.

Later, the physician reviews a consultant's report and communicates a plan. This is one example, not a schedule for all doctors. A hospital-based physician's day could revolve around hospitalized patients, while a surgical day would include different preparation, procedures, and follow-up responsibilities.

## Work Environment and Schedule

Physicians work in offices, hospitals, clinics, and other settings. Some divide their time between patient care and teaching or research. Long hours and on-call work are possible, especially where patients need care around the clock; schedules differ substantially by specialty and employer.

Before choosing a field, ask about the work outside appointments or procedures. Results, documentation, coordination, and coverage arrangements affect a job's rhythm. A specialty name alone will not tell you how predictable a particular position is.

## Skills, Knowledge, and Personal Qualities

A physician needs scientific understanding, careful observation, and the ability to make decisions when information is incomplete. Communication is equally important: a technically accurate explanation is insufficient if the patient cannot understand the choices being discussed.

One useful student exercise is to separate an observation from an interpretation. “The measurement changed” is different from “this is why it changed.” Medicine requires that distinction repeatedly. Teamwork also includes explaining uncertainty, receiving feedback, and sharing information with professionals whose expertise differs from one's own.

## Physical and Emotional Demands

Physical demands vary. Procedures may require sustained concentration and time on one's feet; other work involves examinations, conversation, and computer records. Close patient contact brings infection-control responsibilities.

Emotionally, physicians encounter serious illness, uncertainty, and decisions with substantial consequences. A thoughtful career conversation should include how a team supports difficult decisions and discusses errors or unexpected outcomes. Feeling concern for patients and maintaining clear judgment are both parts of the work; neither eliminates the need for colleagues and organized systems of care.

## Education and Training

The usual pathway includes undergraduate preparation, medical school, and residency, which is supervised training in a medical specialty. The American Medical Association describes residency programs as generally lasting three to seven years, depending on specialty. Some physicians then complete additional fellowship training. Admissions prerequisites, combined programs, and specialty routes make a single total timeline misleading.

DO education includes training in osteopathic principles and the musculoskeletal system as well as physician preparation. DO graduates enter residency and may practice across medical and surgical specialties. Students comparing MD and DO programs should investigate each school's curriculum, clinical opportunities, admissions expectations, and costs.

## Certification and Licensure

A medical degree is not the same as an unrestricted license. Physicians must meet the licensing requirements of the jurisdiction where they practice. Specialty board certification is another professional credential and should not be confused with state licensure.

Kansas regulates physician practice through the State Board of Healing Arts. K.S.A. 65-2803 establishes the requirement for appropriate authorization to practice professions regulated by that board, subject to statutory exceptions. Applicants should follow the board's current instructions for their education, examination, and application route.

## Salary, Entry-Level Earnings, and Job Outlook

BLS reports a **national median annual wage of $275,930 for physicians and surgeons in May 2025**. That is a broad occupational-group figure. It is not a resident's salary, a verified starting salary, or the pay for every specialty. Comparing an individual job with this figure requires attention to specialty, hours, geography, and employment arrangements.

BLS projects **4% employment growth for physicians and surgeons from 2025 to 2035**. The projection describes national employment, not admission chances or a guaranteed opening. Training costs and time also belong in a financial comparison, alongside eventual earnings.

## Specialties and Career Advancement

Specialty training can lead to fields such as family medicine, pediatrics, psychiatry, anesthesiology, or surgery. Further subspecialty preparation may narrow the focus again. These choices change the patients, problems, procedures, and pace of practice.

Physicians may later add teaching, research, or leadership responsibilities. Advancement is not necessarily a move away from patients: developing expertise in a clinical problem can be a career goal in itself. AAMC's Careers in Medicine resources provide a way to investigate specialty differences as students progress through medical training.

## Benefits and Challenges of the Career

The profession offers intellectually demanding work and opportunities to help people through significant moments in their lives. The appeal may lie in a long relationship, solving an unfamiliar problem, or mastering a particular kind of treatment.

The commitments are substantial: prolonged preparation, accountability, and work that cannot always be made predictable. A useful self-assessment considers whether the process of learning medicine and caring for patients interests you, not only the status or income associated with becoming a doctor.

## Career Connections and Related Occupations

Physicians collaborate with nurses, nurse practitioners, physician assistants, pharmacists, therapists, technologists, and many others. These professions have distinct preparation and responsibilities. A patient's care often depends on how well information moves among them.

Compare medicine with another career by tracing a single problem: who gathers information, who develops a diagnosis or treatment plan, and who delivers particular parts of care? The answer will change by setting, but it reveals why a healthcare team contains several professions rather than interchangeable titles.

## Could This Career Interest You?

Are you interested in both scientific reasoning and conversations with people whose needs may be complicated? Can you imagine continuing to study after a difficult problem has resisted the first explanation?

In an informational interview, ask a physician which tasks students rarely see and how their specialty changed their working day. If observation is available through an approved program, respect patient privacy and participation rules. Seeing more than one specialty can help separate interest in medicine generally from interest in a particular kind of practice.

## Professional Associations

The **American Medical Association (AMA)** offers information about physician training and professional practice. The **American Association of Colleges of Osteopathic Medicine (AACOM)** explains osteopathic medical education. The **Association of American Medical Colleges (AAMC)** provides resources for prospective medical students and specialty exploration.

These organizations have different educational and professional roles. Their resources can guide exploration, while state licensing boards determine legal authorization to practice.
