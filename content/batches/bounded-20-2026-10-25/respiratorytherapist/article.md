# Respiratory Therapist

## Career Overview

Respiratory therapists help people whose lungs or breathing cannot meet their needs. A newborn with immature lungs, an adult with a severe breathing crisis, and someone learning to manage chronic lung disease may all receive respiratory care. The work connects knowledge of the heart and lungs with close observation of the person receiving treatment.

An RT may manage breathing-support equipment, perform lung-function tests, deliver prescribed respiratory treatments, and teach patients how to use their equipment. Technology is central, but the purpose is patient care: a machine reading matters because it helps the team understand what is happening to a person. [AARC](https://www.aarc.org/your-rt-career/what-is-an-rt/)

## Duties and Responsibilities

Respiratory therapists assess breathing, review relevant clinical information, and communicate changes to the healthcare team. They check and operate equipment, deliver therapies within the care plan, and document the patient's response. Ventilators support breathing when patients cannot breathe adequately on their own; managing that support requires continuing assessment rather than simply switching on a machine.

Other assignments include pulmonary-function testing, airway care, and teaching patients to use inhaled treatments or home respiratory equipment. Therapists must recognize when a result or a change in condition needs prompt attention. Their work combines hands-on procedures, equipment checks, patient education and consultation. [Mayo Clinic](https://college.mayo.edu/academics/explore-health-care-careers/careers-a-z/respiratory-therapist/)

## A Day in the Life

Consider an illustrative hospital shift. An RT receives a handoff about patients needing breathing support, reviews orders and checks the equipment assigned to those patients. At one bedside, the therapist assesses how a patient is tolerating support and shares observations with the nurse and physician. At another, the task is helping someone understand an inhaled treatment.

An urgent call can interrupt this planned sequence. After responding with the team, the therapist returns to reassess other patients, record care and prepare the next handoff. A pulmonary-function laboratory would have a different rhythm, with scheduled tests and careful coaching so that each patient's effort produces useful measurements.

## Work Environment and Schedule

Hospitals employ many respiratory therapists, including in intensive care and emergency departments. Other settings include outpatient care, rehabilitation and home-based services. AARC also describes roles in transport, research, management and teaching.

Hospital coverage may include nights, weekends and holidays. An outpatient testing or education role may follow an appointment schedule. Before choosing a workplace, consider whether its main work is emergency support, repeated diagnostic testing, or helping people live with long-term disease. Each uses respiratory knowledge in a different way.

## Skills, Knowledge, and Personal Qualities

Anatomy and physiology explain how breathing brings oxygen into the body and removes carbon dioxide. Mathematics, scientific reasoning and technical confidence help therapists interpret measurements and use equipment accurately. They need to notice when a patient's appearance and an instrument reading do not seem to agree.

Listening and clear explanations are just as important. Someone struggling to breathe may be frightened and unable to manage a long conversation. A therapist must communicate calmly, observe carefully and share concise, useful information with colleagues. These skills develop through supervised practice as well as classroom learning.

## Physical and Emotional Demands

RTs may spend substantial time standing, moving between patients and helping with positioning. Close contact with respiratory secretions and ill patients makes infection-prevention procedures and protective equipment part of clinical work. Equipment and patient handling require safe techniques and help from colleagues when needed.

Severe breathing distress can make the work emotionally intense. A therapist may support a family through an uncertain situation while continuing exact technical tasks. The emotional demands differ between critical care and scheduled testing; neither setting removes the responsibility to notice problems and respond appropriately.

## Education and Training

A common entry route is an associate or bachelor's degree in respiratory therapy or respiratory care, including supervised clinical learning. Mayo Clinic describes typical degree pathways taking about two to four years; prerequisites and program organization can change the total time for an individual student. CoARC accreditation is an important consideration when selecting professional preparation.

High-school biology, chemistry and mathematics provide useful foundations. During professional education, students connect heart-and-lung science with assessment, equipment operation and patient care. Clinical experience teaches them to explain a treatment, recognize a change in breathing and work within a team, not merely recall definitions.

## Certification and Licensure

The National Board for Respiratory Care awards national credentials, including Registered Respiratory Therapist (RRT). Its current RRT page describes the Therapist Multiple-Choice and Clinical Simulation examinations and eligibility routes. It also announces an examination transition effective January 2027, so students should use the requirements for their actual graduation and examination dates rather than assume today's sequence will remain unchanged.

State licensure is separate from national credentialing. The Kansas State Board of Healing Arts identifies respiratory therapy as a regulated profession and provides its licensing process. Meeting NBRC requirements does not by itself complete a Kansas license application. [NBRC](https://www.nbrc.org/examinations/rrt/) · [Kansas Board](https://www.ksbha.ks.gov/professions/respiratory-therapist-r-t)

## Salary, Entry-Level Earnings, and Job Outlook

BLS reports a **U.S. median annual wage of $82,280 in May 2025** for respiratory therapists. A median is the midpoint of workers' wages, not a promised first-job salary. BLS projects **9% employment growth during 2025–2035**, with about **8,600 openings per year**, including replacement openings.

A first offer depends on employer, location, experience, schedule and compensation structure. Compare current local postings and whether overtime, shift differentials and benefits are included when evaluating offers. The national statistic does not establish a Kansas starting wage. [BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/respiratory-therapists.htm)

## Specialties and Career Advancement

Therapists may develop expertise in neonatal or pediatric care, adult critical care, pulmonary diagnostics, rehabilitation or transport. Specialization changes the patients, equipment and clinical situations encountered; it is not simply a more advanced job title.

Experience and further education can support work in department leadership, clinical education, research or equipment-related roles. A therapist interested in teaching may enjoy helping students connect a measured value with what they observe at the bedside. Someone drawn to operations may instead focus on staffing, quality and dependable service delivery.

## Benefits and Challenges of the Career

The profession offers direct involvement in a basic human need: breathing. Some encounters involve urgent stabilization; others involve teaching that helps someone manage treatment outside a hospital. The combination of patient relationships and technical problem-solving can be appealing.

The same responsibilities bring challenges. Machines require attention, clinical priorities can change quickly, and difficult outcomes occur despite careful care. For a student considering the field, the useful question is whether both its patient contact and its precise technical work are attractive.

## Career Connections and Related Occupations

RTs work with physicians, nurses and other clinicians. A nurse coordinates many aspects of a patient's care; the RT contributes concentrated expertise in respiratory assessment and treatment. A pulmonologist is a physician whose medical training and responsibilities differ from those of an RT.

Compared with imaging careers, respiratory therapy often involves repeated treatment and reassessment rather than producing a diagnostic image. Compared with physical therapy, it concentrates on breathing and cardiopulmonary support rather than a broad movement-rehabilitation program. These differences can help students identify which part of healthcare draws their interest.

## Could This Career Interest You?

Would you enjoy learning why a breathing measurement changes and then checking what that means for a patient? Can you picture explaining unfamiliar equipment to someone who feels anxious? Are you interested in both science and direct care?

Explore a hospital RT's work alongside pulmonary testing or rehabilitation. Ask how much of the day involves urgent calls, education, documentation and equipment. That comparison is more useful than assuming every RT works in an intensive care unit.

## Professional Associations

The [American Association for Respiratory Care (AARC)](https://www.aarc.org/your-rt-career/be-an-rt/) provides career exploration, educational resources and professional community. Its specialty and career resources show how the profession extends beyond one hospital unit.

AARC membership, an NBRC credential and a state license serve different purposes. The association supports learning and professional connections; it does not replace the credentialing or licensing process.
